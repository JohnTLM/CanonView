## 2. Core theses

1. **Only own-nature is negated, never the world.** "What he refutes is not the world but one picture of it: a world of things complete in themselves, which then come into relation." (Intro) Following Tsongkhapa: "what reasoning negates is arising established by its own nature, not mere arising as the world speaks of it" (ch. 1; repeated in ch. 2, 3 and 20).
2. **Svabhāva is defined as unmade and independent, so nothing that appears could have it.** "The argument is not a survey that happens to find no essences. It shows that nothing which appears could have one." (ch. 15) This is why the books call the conclusion "a logical necessity".
3. **Dependent arising is emptiness.** 24.18 gives "four names for one fact". Emptiness is itself "a designation in dependence" and "cannot become a new ground".
4. **Negation is non-implicative (prasajya-pratiṣedha).** Rejecting the four options of 1.1 "is not a fifth theory of arising" (ch. 1). The same holds for 6.10, 9.12, 22.15 ("rules out decay without installing permanence") and 25 ("refusing all four is not a fifth theory of nirvāṇa").
5. **"Not found" is not "does not happen".** "'Not found' is the key phrase" (ch. 2). Nāgārjuna is contrasted with Zeno: motion is not shown to be an illusion.
6. **Paired terms are designated together, with neither first.** This covers condition and effect, walker and walking, seer and seen, mark and marked, agent and action (8.12) and fire and fuel. "Mutual dependence is not a circle that needs a first link; it is what being designated together means" (ch. 10).
7. **Self-standing things could be neither one nor two.** The test is in 2.21, 6.10, 10.1, 14.8, 18.10 and 20.19–20. "Something that can be established neither as one with its partner nor as different from it cannot be established as a thing at all" (ch. 2).
8. **Only an empty world can work.** "For whom emptiness is possible, everything is possible" (24.14). Own-nature would make the world "fixed like an anvil" (24.38): "A substance would not be a firmer foundation. It would be a stopped clock." (ch. 24)
9. **The two truths are one appearance taken two ways, not two layers.** "They are one appearance, taken in two ways." "Convention is the ladder; the ultimate is where it leads; and the ladder is not thrown away, because the ultimate is only ever the emptiness of conventional things." (ch. 24)
10. **Emptiness is a remedy, not a view or a ground.** 13.8 uses the purgative image. "This is why the books keep warning against a 'substrate of emptiness': a hidden openness, potential or awareness behind appearances" (ch. 13). "Emptiness is itself empty."
11. **The person remains as the mere I.** The self is neither the aggregates nor other than them (18.1, 27.4–8), "yet it is not non-existent either" (27.8). Tsongkhapa's mere I "acts, remembers and keeps promises" (ch. 9, 18).
12. **There is continuity without a carrier.** 18.10 is "neither cut off nor permanent": rice seed and rice plant. Nāgasena's flame and the lamp of 27.22 make the same point. "Continuity is real and designated; a carrier is not found" (ch. 16).
13. **Karma works because it is empty.** "Own-nature would not protect karma; it would destroy it. A karma that works must be empty" (17.22–25). The magician's creations (17.31–33): "empty things really act on empty things, and … this is the only acting there is."
14. **Saṃsāra and nirvāṇa are not two places.** They share one limit (25.19–20). "What changes is the grasping, not the world." In convention they stay "clearly distinct: one is bondage, the other is freedom" (ch. 25).
15. **The root of suffering is a way of construing, not a substance.** 18.5 runs: action and afflictions ← vikalpa ← prapañca, and "Elaboration stops in emptiness."
16. **The Buddha's nature is the world's nature, which is having none.** 22.16: "There is no special ultimate reserved for him."
17. **Even the corrections (impermanence, no-self) are medicine.** Held as the real natures of real things, "they would be one more mistake" (ch. 23). "No-self" is "only a designation" (18.6).
18. **The extremes share a refused presupposition.** Permanence and annihilation are "two answers to one question: what happens to a thing with own-nature?" (15.10–11) "Logic has not been suspended; the question had nothing to be about."
19. **No one owns suffering, so it can change from many sides.** "The way out of suffering opens exactly because no one, and nothing, owns it from its own side" (ch. 12).

### Per-chapter reading (one line each)

1. Conditions and effects are found only together; arising "taken as something a thing does from its own side" is not found.
2. Motion is "not found as a thing of its own"; walker, walking and path are designated together.
3. Seeing is "a meeting" (eye, light, object, attention); seer, seeing and seen are "one occurrence, named three ways".
4. Form and its cause are "one relation named from two sides"; the aggregates are designated too, and nothing is a final building block.
5. Space is "room for something", designated with its mark; it is neither thing nor absence.
6. Desire and the desirous are neither together nor apart. They are designated in dependence, so desire "can loosen".
7. Arising, abiding and ceasing are designations within before and after, not forces. The unconditioned falls with the conditioned, so nirvāṇa is not "a second, better realm".
8. Agent and action are each established only in dependence on the other (8.12). Responsibility "works better without" a fixed agent.
9. There is no owner-self prior to experience. Seer and seeing are designated together, and the conceptions "exists" and "does not exist" fall away.
10. Fire and fuel are "one burning, described from two sides". The self, the pot and the cloth are the same.
11. No first limit is found for anything. Birth and death are "two ends of one designation, a life".
12. Suffering is not made by self, other, both or no cause. It is dependently arisen and so changeable.
13. Conditioned things deceive by seeming self-standing. There is change without a changer, and emptiness is not a view.
14. Otherness depends on the other. Interaction needs "neither a gap nor a fusion".
15. Own-nature is unmade and independent, so nothing has it. Is and is-not both fall, and a world of essences "could not move".
16. No wanderer is found, and liberation is not a possession. "Nothing added, nothing removed."
17. Karma is empty and therefore works. Action is "imperishable" because it never arose with a nature of its own.
18. The mere I remains. Elaboration stops in emptiness, and what arises dependently is neither the same nor different.
19. Time is "designated on change". There is no container or river of time.
20. A collection of conditions fails just as single conditions do. Production is "a designation that works".
21. Arising and passing are named together. Belief in real existence forces the choice between permanence and annihilation, and the continuum of becoming is not found as a thing that flows.
22. The Tathāgata is not found in the fivefold search. "Empty" is spoken only for designation, and his nature is the world's lack of nature.
23. The afflictions are empty, which is why they can be abandoned. Attractive and unattractive are designated together.
24. Emptiness secures the Four Noble Truths and own-nature destroys them. Dependent arising = emptiness = dependent designation = middle way.
25. Nirvāṇa is neither thing nor absence. It is "this world, no longer misread".
26. The twelve links are the conventional account, "fully endorsed". Emptiness is why the chain can end.
27. Views about past and future self and world rest on a self or world with own-nature. All views are let go, which means letting go of "views that grasp at things as existing from their own side".

### How key verses are rendered

- **1.1:** "Not from itself, not from another, not from both, and not without a cause: no thing anywhere, of any kind, is ever found to have arisen."
- **15.10:** "'It is' is the grasping of permanence. 'It is not' is the view of annihilation. So the wise should not take their stand on existence or on non-existence."
- **15.11:** "'What exists by its own nature does not cease to exist': that is permanence. 'It existed before and does not exist now': that brings annihilation."
- **18.9:** "Not known by depending on another, at peace, not elaborated by elaborations, free of conceptual construction, without plurality: this is the mark of reality." The book adds: "The marks are negative… Nāgārjuna does not install a positive absolute behind appearances."
- **24.18:** "Dependent arising is what we call emptiness. That is a designation in dependence, and that itself is the middle way."
- **24.19:** "Since there is no phenomenon that has not arisen in dependence, there is no phenomenon that is not empty."
- **25.19:** "There is nothing at all that sets saṃsāra apart from nirvāṇa. There is nothing at all that sets nirvāṇa apart from saṃsāra."
- **25.20:** "The limit of nirvāṇa and the limit of the round of saṃsāra: between these two, not even the finest gap is found."
- Also notable:
  - **24.14:** "For whom emptiness is possible, everything is possible. For whom emptiness is not possible, nothing is possible." (yujyate = "is possible", "fits", "makes sense", "works")
  - **18.10:** "Whatever comes to be in dependence on something is not that very thing, and it is not other than it either. So it is neither cut off nor permanent."
  - **13.8:** "The victorious ones taught emptiness as the way out of all views. But those for whom emptiness is a view, they called incurable."
  - **22.16:** "…The Tathāgata has no nature of his own; this world has no nature of its own."
  - **25.24:** "The quieting of all taking-as-objects, the quieting of elaborations, is peace. No teaching was ever taught by the Buddha to anyone, anywhere."
  - **18.5** ends: "Elaboration stops in emptiness." Candrakīrti reads śūnyatāyām; some moderns read śūnyatayā.

## 3. Key terms

The glossary is reproduced in full in the Appendix with the book's exact spellings. Points of usage:

- **Own-nature (svabhāva):** the standard English rendering throughout. "Essence" appears as a secondary gloss in ch. 15. The book never says "inherent existence" as a term; it uses "from its own side" and "self-standing".
- **Other-nature (parabhāva).**
- **Nature (prakṛti):** synonym of own-nature in 15.8–9.
- **Emptiness (śūnyatā):** "the absence of own-nature in anything; not a stuff, not a ground" (ch. 13), and "itself empty, never a thing or a ground" (ch. 24).
- **Dependent arising (pratītyasamutpāda):** "things appearing only in dependence on other things, and on the way they are picked out."
- **Designation family:**
  - designation (prajñapti)
  - designated (prajñapta)
  - dependent designation (prajñaptir upādāya)
  - made known (ajyate, prajñapyate)
  - verb phrases: "designated together", "designated in dependence"
- **Upādāna** is glossed several ways: grasping (ch. 3, 8), taking-up (ch. 16), taking up/appropriation (ch. 22) and what is taken up (ch. 10, 27). Related: upādātṛ, "the one who takes up".
- **Elaboration (prapañca)** and its quieting (prapañcopaśama). Also: conceptual construction (vikalpa), conceptual thinking (saṃkalpa), conceptions (kalpanā), taking-as-object (upalambha).
- **Non-implicative negation (prasajya-pratiṣedha).** Non-arising (anutpāda).
- **Mere I:** given with Tibetan "nga tsam" and attributed to Tsongkhapa.
- **Personal and doctrinal names** (exact spellings):
  - Personalists (Pudgalavāda) / Pudgalavādins
  - Sarvāstivāda, Sautrāntikas, Sāṃmitīyas
  - Kātyāyana (Pāli Kaccānagotta; SN 12.15)
  - Vacchagotta (MN 72), Anurādha (SN 22.86)
  - Nāgasena, Candrakīrti, Tsongkhapa, Buddhaghosa, Gautama
  - Śālistamba Sūtra
- **Spelling conventions:**
  - Sanskrit is given with full diacritics: saṃsāra, nirvāṇa, Tathāgata, Prāsaṅgika, Mādhyamika, Mūlamadhyamakakārikā.
  - "Abhidharma", "Madhyamaka" and "Dharma"/"dharma" appear in both cases. "Sangha" has no diacritic.
  - The Pāli "Anatta" is used only in the companion book's title.
  - Tibetan "nga tsam" is unitalicised plain text.

## 4. Rejected views

- **Own-nature / essence / substance:**
  - It is defined so that nothing which appears could meet it (15.1–2).
  - It is rejected because it would freeze the world (13.4, 15.8–9, 24.16–38).
  - It would make the afflictions impossible to abandon (23.24).
  - It would make karma unmade (17.22).
- **Nihilism / annihilation / emptiness as nothing:**
  - The opponent of 24 "takes 'empty' to mean 'non-existent'". This is one wrong end of the snake (24.11).
  - Absence is always dependent on presence (5.6, 25.7–8).
  - 23.25 rejects reading "unreal by own nature" as "nothing at all".
- **Eternalism / permanence:** 15.10–11, 21.14, 27.15. Both extremes start from "a thing with a nature of its own".
- **Emptiness as a thing, ground or substrate:**
  - "Grasped as a thing, an ultimate essence or cosmic ground, it becomes a new substance" (24.11).
  - The "substrate of emptiness: a hidden openness, potential or awareness behind appearances" (ch. 13).
  - 13.7 rejects emptiness as "a stuff that all things are made of".
  - 13.3 rejects the opponent's turning of emptiness into a property with a real bearer.
- **A prior self, owner or witness:**
  - Ch. 9 rejects "a self that is the witness of experience" (the ātman of non-Buddhist schools) and "the owner behind the windows".
  - Argument: such a self has nothing to reveal it, and if it can stand without experience, experience can stand without it.
- **The Pudgalavāda person:** the fire-and-fuel analogy "does not show a self that is real in its own right" (ch. 10).
- **Abhidharma dharmas as final building blocks:** "the self would be gone, and a new set of substances would have taken its place" (ch. 4).
  - The three marks as real forces (ch. 7).
  - Sarvāstivāda's existence in three times (ch. 19).
  - Space as unconditioned (ch. 5).
- **Doing without a doer (Buddhaghosa's asymmetry):** in-the-books note to ch. 8. Nāgārjuna "refuses even that asymmetry". Ch. 8: "It does not say the action is real and the agent is a mere label, as if only doing exists."
- **Nirvāṇa as a second realm or place:** 7.33, 16.10, 25.
- **An egoless person as a new self:** 18.3, "Liberation does not produce a refined self that has the quality of selflessness."
- **Time as a container or river:** ch. 19. **Space as a self-standing container:** ch. 5.
- **The traveller / invariant bearer across lives:** 16.3, 21.18–21, 27. Also both school models of karma taken as real (the continuum; the imperishable as a lasting record).
- **Causal power handed over:** baton, "passing over" (20.5–9). "The 'power to produce' is not found as a separate thing" (1.4).
- **A fourth state "neither thing nor absence":** 25.15–16.
- **The Tathāgata as a deeper reality behind the world** (22.16). The opposite reading ("just one more ordinary thing") is also rejected.
- **Zeno's reading of motion as illusion** (ch. 2).

## 6. Practice and realisation

- **Insight is checked in experience.** "The verses were written to be tested, not believed." (Intro) Every chapter ends with a "try it" exercise: hear a sound fade, walk, look for the looker, hold a cup, watch irritation.
- **The relation to anatta (stages).**
  - "Many people meet this first in practice, as the discovery that there is seeing without a seer behind it. The chapter adds the second half: the seeing, too, is not found on its own." (ch. 9)
  - Nāgārjuna's "no seeing without a seer" (3.6, 8.12, ch. 9) can seem to undo anatta. It does not: "he follows the opponent's assumptions until they collapse." (ch. 3, 9, citing TAE "The Great Desynchronization")
  - Ch. 4 makes the same step at the level of doctrine: the Abhidharma stopped "one step short".
- **Grasping at the goal.** "'Free of grasping, I shall reach nirvāṇa; nirvāṇa will be mine.'… a great hold of grasping" (16.9).
  - "Effort hardens into grasping at progress and at experiences" (citing *The Logic of Dependency*).
  - "This does not mean practice is useless or that one should not aspire." What frees "is the seeing that the one who grasps and the thing grasped are not found as they are taken to be, not a better object for the same grasping."
- **Practice arranges conditions; it does not fight defects.** "Practice is not an attack on fixed defects but a way of arranging conditions" (ch. 6). Afflictions "are not removed by force, like stones lifted out of a field. They end when the mistake that feeds them is seen through" (ch. 23).
- **The mechanism of release (ch. 18, 23, 26).**
  - When "I" and "mine" are used up, grasping stops (18.4).
  - Vikalpa comes from prapañca, and prapañca stops in emptiness (18.5).
  - "Ignorance ceases through 'cultivating this very knowledge'" (26.11). For Candrakīrti this means seeing dependent arising, which is seeing emptiness.
  - The practical point of the chain is the step from feeling to craving: "That step, from feeling to craving, is where the wheel turns."
- **No subject of freedom remains.** "No subject survives the analysis to stand outside, look back and enjoy its freedom" (ch. 18, citing TAE). "It is the end of looking for a self, including a selfless one."
- **Recognition versus nature.** Saṃsāra and nirvāṇa are equal in nature and distinct in convention, "and the path is what turns the first into the second. The path is not empty words. It is the removal of a misreading." (ch. 25)
  - In the books, Rheomode Synthesis 2 restates 25's shared limit "as a claim about nature, not a claim that recognition is unnecessary".
  - TAE "Resonances" distinguishes "sharing nirvāṇa's nature and having realised it".
  - Talk listed: "Why Insights and Experiences Still Require Right View".
- **Right view is not a view held.** "Right view, the seeing of emptiness, is not one more thing to cling to; it is what loosens the clinging." (ch. 27) "The test of having understood it is not having a new view, but being freed from the need to hold one." (ch. 13)
- **Graded teaching.** 18.6 and 18.8 are read with Candrakīrti as steps for students at different stages.
- **Effortlessness.** The book has no explicit doctrine of effortlessness. The nearest is 25.24: "When that grip relaxes, the spinning stops by itself"; also the homage ("the spinning stops by itself").

## 8. Tensions

**With the canon:**

- **Openness as a property, or as what emptiness is (canon 2, 3).**
  - Ch. 24: "Everything that functions does so by being open to conditions, and being open to conditions is exactly what 'empty of own-nature' means."
  - Ch. 24 closing: "That openness is emptiness, at work in your own life."
  - These equate emptiness with openness as a feature of things, not a mode of seeing.
  - Ch. 5: "Openness, vivid and usable, and no container behind it" (space as openness).
- **Emptiness as enabler or explanation of appearing (canon 3, 6).**
  - Intro: "only an empty world could work at all."
  - Ch. 24: "Only because things are empty can anything arise, change, be practised or be attained."
  - Ch. 26: "Emptiness does not stop the world working; it is why the world can work."
  - Ch. 15: "Because nothing has own-nature, everything can change."
  - These are faithful to 24.14 but phrased as "because/why", which canon 3 says openness does not supply.
- **Freedom as a result or attainment (canon 5).**
  - 24.10 is rendered "Without coming to the ultimate, nirvāṇa is not attained", against 25.3 "not attained".
  - 26.11: "ignorance ceases through cultivating this very knowledge."
  - Ch. 25: "the path is what turns the first into the second."

- **"True nature" or "nature of reality" language (canon 1).**

  - 18.9 gives "the mark of reality", including "at peace".

  - 26 glossary: "How things are (tattva): what knowing sees; for Madhyamaka, emptiness" (risks presenting emptiness as an object known).

## 9. Candidate canon points

1. **Only own-nature is negated.** Reasoning negates arising, going, seeing and selves established by their own nature. It does not negate arising as the world speaks of it. Nothing that works is taken away. What goes is a picture laid over it.
2. **Svabhāva is unmade and independent by definition.** Anything that can be found is already picked out by contrast and designation, so it fails the definition before the search begins. This is a logical necessity, not a survey.
3. **"Not found" is not "does not happen".** Unfindability under analysis is not denial of occurrence. The walker still reaches the window, and the cup still holds the tea.
4. **Paired terms are designated together.** Condition and effect, agent and action, seer and seen, fire and fuel are named in dependence on each other, with neither first. Mutual dependence is not a vicious circle, because neither member existed first to lend existence to the other.
5. **Neither one nor two.** Whatever arises in dependence is neither identical with what it depends on nor other than it. It is therefore neither cut off nor permanent. This is how continuity, karma and the person hold without a carrier.
6. **Dependent arising, emptiness, dependent designation and the middle way are one fact.** Emptiness is itself a designation in dependence on what is empty. It cannot be promoted to a ground.
7. **Emptiness is a remedy, not a view.** It removes grasping at own-nature and is not itself to be held. Held as "the truth", openness, potential or awareness behind appearances, it becomes the incurable illness.
8. **The two truths are one appearance taken two ways.** Working and emptiness are not two facts or two layers. The ultimate is taught only through convention and is only ever the emptiness of conventional things.
9. **Own-nature, not emptiness, would freeze the world.** A thing with its own nature could not arise, change, act, be acted on or cease. Karma, path and liberation require emptiness.
10. **The mere I stands.** The person is designated on the aggregates, identical with none of them and found nowhere apart from them. It still acts, remembers and keeps promises. "No-self" is a corrective designation, not a new fact.
11. **Saṃsāra and nirvāṇa share one limit.** They are not two places. They are equal in nature and distinct in convention as bondage and freedom. What changes is the grasping, not the world.
12. **Correctives are medicine.** Impermanence, no-self and emptiness undo the four inversions. Held as the real natures of real things, they are one more mistake.
13. **Grasping at the goal is great grasping.** "Nirvāṇa will be mine" keeps the self that liberation releases. What frees is seeing that grasper and grasped are not found as taken, not a better object for grasping.

## 11. Nature of mind and phenomena; luminous clarity

Numbered 11 at the coordinator's request; this file has no section 10. I searched the full docx for the following terms: lumin-, clarity, clear light, 'od gsal, prabhāsvara, rigpa, awareness, presence, vivid, witness, ground, nature of mind, rangtong, shentong, "empty of other", Nyingma, Dzogchen, Mipham, Longchenpa, kadag, lhundrub, primordial, buddha-nature. **Result: the book has no passage on luminosity or clarity at all.** None of "luminous", "luminosity", "clarity", "clear light", "'od gsal", "prabhāsvara", "rigpa", "rangtong", "shentong", "Nyingma", "Dzogchen", "Longchenpa", "kadag", "lhundrub" or "buddha-nature" occurs. The book stays strictly within Prāsaṅgika reading of the MMK.

### (a) Nature of mind and nature of phenomena

The book never sets the nature of mind against the nature of phenomena. Mind and its contents are treated as empty in exactly the same way as everything else:

- **Ch. 24, on 24.19:** "There is no phenomenon that has not arisen in dependence. So there is no phenomenon that is not empty. … Emptiness is not a special region of reality, reserved for the enlightened. It is the condition of every cup, thought and breath." This explicitly puts thought on a par with cup and breath.
- **Ch. 5, on 5.7:** "The other elements, earth, water, fire, air and consciousness, are just like it [space]. … The same for heat and wetness and motion, and for consciousness and its mark of knowing." Consciousness and its defining mark, knowing, are designated together, like space and non-obstruction.
- **Ch. 5, on 5.6:** "To call space a 'thing' or a 'nothing', one would need a viewpoint outside both, from which to inspect and classify. No such viewpoint is found. Knowing, too, is part of what depends."
- **Ch. 3:**
  - Glossary entry: "Consciousness (vijñāna): awareness of an object, arising in dependence on a faculty and its field."
  - "In its place is seeing as a meeting: eye, light, object and attention arising together, with seer, seeing and seen named from that one event."
- **Ch. 22, on 22.16:** "The Buddha and the world are empty in exactly the same way. The emptiness found by searching for the Buddha is the same emptiness found by searching for a cup, a chariot or a self. There is no special ultimate reserved for him." Also: "He and the world share one 'nature', which is having none. The dashed box is not a substrate under both; it marks what is not found in either."
- **Ch. 9:** "Many people meet this first in practice, as the discovery that there is seeing without a seer behind it. The chapter adds the second half: the seeing, too, is not found on its own." Experience itself, not only its owner, is empty.
- **Ch. 26 / glossary:** "How things are (tattva): what knowing sees; for Madhyamaka, emptiness." This is the nearest the book comes to a "knowing" that sees reality. Emptiness is put as what is seen, not as a property of the knowing.

### (b) Awareness, presence, vividness, witness

**Awareness** appears only as an aggregate or ordinary function, never as a ground:

- "a Tathāgata with no body, no feeling, no perception, no will and no awareness. No such being is ever met." (ch. 22)
- "a body, feelings, perceptions, habits, awareness, the five aggregates" (ch. 27)
- "Try to locate it, as distinct from the sensations, thoughts and awareness going on now, and nothing is there to point to." (ch. 27)

**Awareness as a ground is explicitly rejected:** "This is why the books keep warning against a 'substrate of emptiness': a hidden openness, potential or awareness behind appearances, to which emptiness is quietly promoted. The view of emptiness is the most refined form of the habit the whole text is dismantling. Emptiness is itself empty." (ch. 13)

**The witness is rejected:**

- "Non-Buddhist schools spoke of an ātman, a self that is the witness of experience." (ch. 9)
- "you do not find a witness standing apart" (ch. 9)
- Glossary: "Self (ātman): in this chapter, an owner and witness of experience, existing from its own side."

**Vividness** is always a mode of appearing that is empty, never a property or ground:

- "The walking is vivid and exact. The walker, as something apart from it, is not found." (ch. 2)
- "You find the tree, vivid, and the seeing of it, but no viewer standing apart from the view." (ch. 3)
- "Openness, vivid and usable, and no container behind it." (ch. 5, of space)
- "what happens, happens the way a dream happens: fully, vividly, with effects, and without anything that arises, lasts or ends from its own side." (ch. 7)
- City of gandharvas: "what appears vividly and is not found when sought." (ch. 7 glossary)
- "It is vivid, and it is not found apart from its conditions" (ch. 25, irritation)
- In-the-books pointer to LN ch. 5, "Conventional Truth: Vividness and Functional Efficacy". Vividness is placed on the conventional side.

**Presence** appears only as the opposite of absence ("Absence depends on presence just as presence depends on its marks", ch. 5), apart from three titles:

- The YouTube title "MMK Non-Implicative Negation ironically shows: Dependent Arising = Spontaneous Presence" (ch. 1 and 15). "Spontaneous presence" is the usual English for lhundrub, but the term is not used or explained in the text.
- TAE title "The Starting Point: The Journey Through Presence" (ch. 9 pointer).
- LN chapter "States of Awareness, Deep Sleep, and Death" (title only).

**"At peace" and the mark of reality are framed negatively:**

- 7.16: "'At peace' (śānta) means quiet, empty of own-nature: not stirred by an arising of its own."
- 18.9: "The marks are negative, and even 'at peace' means that something has been stilled. Reality is described by what is absent from it… Nāgārjuna does not install a positive absolute behind appearances."
- 18.7: the "true nature of things (dharmatā), unarisen and unceasing, is like nirvāṇa. This is not a mystical realm beyond the world."

### (c) Rangtong, shentong, Nyingma, Dzogchen, Mipham, Longchenpa, kadag/lhundrub

- **Mipham** appears only in two YouTube talk titles:
  - "The Paradox of the Phantom Vase: Mipham vs. Gelug on Emptiness & the Negandum" (ch. 13)
  - "Mipham's 2 Models of 2 Truth", "on the two truths of 24.8–10" (ch. 24)
- **Not named anywhere:** rangtong, shentong, Nyingma, Dzogchen, Longchenpa, kadag. lhundrub appears only via the "Spontaneous Presence" title above.
- **The stated lineage is Prāsaṅgika (Candrakīrti, Tsongkhapa), not Nyingma.** The author's Dzogchen stance on luminous clarity is therefore absent from this book. The book is purely rangtong in effect, though the word is not used.
- **Shentong-leaning sentences: none found.** Nothing presents luminosity, awareness or emptiness as a truly existing ground or as "empty of other". The text explicitly blocks this in three places:
  - 13 (the "substrate of emptiness")
  - 24.11 ("an ultimate essence or cosmic ground")
  - 24.18 ("cannot become a new ground")
- **Lines a shentong reader could use** (flagged as soft, not as leaning):
  - "the true nature of things (dharmatā), unarisen and unceasing, is like nirvāṇa" (18.7)
  - "this is the mark of reality" (18.9)
  - "Buddha… beyond elaboration and does not decay (avyaya)" (22.15)
  - Each is immediately glossed negatively. For 22.15: "The phrase rules out decay without installing permanence. This is a non-implicative negation."
- **Gap for the canon:** this book supplies the emptiness side only. The Dzogchen account of luminous clarity (as empty, as a mode of appearing) must come from the other books. It cannot be cross-checked here.

- Permanence (śāśvata): the extreme of 'it is', a thing that never stops being what it is. (Ch. 15, 21)
- Permanence and annihilation (śāśvata, uccheda): the two extremes about the self, lasting for ever or wiped out. (Ch. 27)
- Person (pudgala): the one designated in dependence on the aggregates. (Ch. 12, 16)
- Personalists (Pudgalavāda): an early school that held the person to be real, neither the same as nor different from the aggregates. (Ch. 10)
- Prior entity (prāg vyavasthito bhāvaḥ, 'the entity in place before'): a self held to exist before, and apart from, the experiences it owns. (Ch. 9)
- Quieting (upaśama): the settling of 'is' and 'is not' when no thing with its own status is found. (Ch. 5)
- Reality (tattva): things as they are, described in verse 18.9 by what is absent from them. (Ch. 18)
- Really existing (sadbhūta): existing already, complete and on its own. Its opposite is asadbhūta, not existing. (Ch. 8)
- Same or different (ekatva, anyatva): if two things with their own natures were related, they would have to be one or the other. (Ch. 2)
- Saṃsāra 'wandering on', the round of birth and death driven by grasping. (Ch. 16, 25)
- Seeing (darśana): here, the eye as the power to see. (Ch. 3)
- Seeing, hearing and the rest (darśana, śravaṇa): the activities of the senses. (Ch. 9)
- Seer, seeing, seen (draṣṭṛ, darśana, draṣṭavya): the three parts of a moment of seeing. (Ch. 14)
- Seer, seen (draṣṭṛ, draṣṭavya): the one who sees and what is seen. (Ch. 3)
- Self (ātman): in this chapter, an owner and witness of experience, existing from its own side. (Ch. 9, 18)
- Selfless (anātman): without a self, without own-nature. (Ch. 22)
- Sense fields (āyatana): the senses and their objects. (Ch. 14)
- Separate (pṛthak, pṛthaktva): apart, distinct. (Ch. 6)
- Space (ākāśa): known by its mark of not obstructing; some schools held it to be unconditioned. (Ch. 5)
- Stream, continuum (saṃtāna): a series of moments, each arising as the one before passes away. (Ch. 21)
- Suffering (duḥkha): unease and pain in all forms, from plain hurt to the unsatisfactoriness of what changes. (Ch. 12)
- Taking up (upādāna): what a person depends on, as a fire takes up fuel; also 'appropriation'. (Ch. 22)
- Taking-as-object (upalambha): the grip that turns an appearance into a thing to hold. (Ch. 25)
- Taking-up, grasping (upādāna): the holding-on by which a life and a self are taken up again and again. (Ch. 16)
- Tathāgata 'the one thus gone' or 'thus come', a title of the Buddha. (Ch. 22, 25)
- The desirous one (rakta): the one who desires; literally 'one who is coloured' by passion. (Ch. 6)
- The eight extremes arising and ceasing, permanence and annihilation, coming and going, identity and difference. (Ch. 1)
- The fivefold search same, different, possessor, container, contained. Candrakīrti's chariot adds two more to make seven. (Ch. 10)
- The four great elements (mahābhūta): earth, water, fire and air, taken as the cause of form. (Ch. 4)
- The four noble truths (ārya-satya): suffering, its origin, its cessation and the path to its cessation. (Ch. 24)
- The four ways (cāturvidhya): from self, from other, from both, without cause. (Ch. 12)
- The imperishable (avipraṇāśa): the second school's lasting record of an action, like a promissory note. (Ch. 17)
- The middle way (madhyamā pratipad): free of the extremes of existence by own-nature and of nothingness. (Ch. 24)
- The one who takes up (upādātṛ): the self, designated in dependence on what it takes up. (Ch. 27)
- The round of birth and death (saṃsāra): the ongoing cycle of lives driven by craving and ignorance. (Ch. 11)
- The three jewels (triratna): the Buddha, the Dharma and the Sangha. (Ch. 24)
- The three marks (trilakṣaṇa): arising (utpāda), abiding (sthiti) and ceasing (bhaṅga or nirodha), held to define the conditioned. (Ch. 7)
- The three times past (atīta), present (pratyutpanna) and future (anāgata). (Ch. 19)
- The twelve links (dvādaśāṅga pratītyasamutpāda): the chain from ignorance to ageing and death. (Ch. 26)
- Thing and absence (bhāva, abhāva): presence and lack. An absence is always the absence of something. (Ch. 5)
- Thing, entity (bhāva): a thing taken as existing from its own side; time is designated on such things, which are themselves not found. (Ch. 19)
- Time (kāla): here, time taken as a thing in its own right, a container or a flow. (Ch. 19)
- Together (saha, sahabhāva): being with, existing alongside. (Ch. 6)
- Ultimate truth (paramārtha-satya): what analysis finds when it seeks a thing's own nature: emptiness. (Ch. 24)
- Unconditioned (asaṃskṛta): in the Abhidharma, what has no arising or ceasing, such as space and nirvāṇa. (Ch. 7)
- Undeclared questions (avyākṛta): questions the Buddha set aside, such as whether the Tathāgata exists after death. (Ch. 22)
- View (dṛṣṭi): a position held as true, especially about what things are in themselves. (Ch. 13, 27)
- What is taken up (upādāna): the aggregates that the self is designated in dependence on. (Ch. 10, 27)
- Without beginning or end (anavarāgra): having no first or last limit that can be found. (Ch. 11)
